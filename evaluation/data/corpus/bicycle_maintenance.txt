Bicycle Tyres and Punctures

Correct tyre pressure makes a bicycle faster, more comfortable and less likely to suffer punctures. Check the pressure at least once a week with a pump that has a gauge, because inner tubes slowly lose air even when they are not damaged. The recommended pressure range is printed on the sidewall of the tyre. Riders who are heavier or carry loads usually need pressure near the top of the range.

A tyre with too little air can be squeezed against the rim when the wheel hits a sharp edge such as a pothole or curb. This usually cuts two small parallel holes in the inner tube, a type of damage called a pinch flat or snake bite.

To fix a puncture, first remove the wheel from the frame. Use plastic tyre levers to lift one side of the tyre over the rim and pull out the inner tube. Find the hole by pumping some air into the tube and listening for a hiss, or by holding the tube under water and watching for bubbles. Roughen the area around the hole with sandpaper, apply a thin layer of rubber cement, wait until it becomes tacky and press a patch firmly over the hole.

Before putting the tube back, run your fingers carefully around the inside of the tyre to find the thorn, glass or wire that caused the puncture. If it is left in place, it will puncture the repaired tube again. Many riders carry a spare tube so they can replace it at the roadside and patch the damaged tube later at home.

Chain and Drivetrain Care

The chain transfers power from the pedals to the rear wheel, and it works best when it is clean and lubricated. Clean and lubricate the chain every few hundred kilometers, and always after riding in rain or on muddy roads. Apply one drop of lubricant to each roller while turning the pedals backward, then wipe off the excess with a clean rag. Lubricant left on the outside of the chain attracts dirt and grit, which turns into a grinding paste.

As a chain is used, the pins and rollers wear and the chain gradually becomes longer. This is often called chain stretch, although the metal plates do not actually stretch. A worn chain no longer fits the teeth of the cassette and chainrings properly and wears them out quickly.

A chain wear checker is a simple tool that drops between the rollers to measure elongation. Replace the chain when it reaches 0.5 percent elongation on bicycles with eleven or more rear sprockets, or 0.75 percent on bicycles with fewer gears. Replacing a chain on time is much cheaper than replacing a worn cassette and chainrings as well.

A badly worn drivetrain often shows itself when the chain skips or slips over the teeth of the rear sprockets while you pedal hard, for example when climbing or starting from a stop. Gears that change slowly or noisily may instead need a small adjustment of the derailleur cable tension.

Brakes and Lights

Brakes should be checked before every ride. Squeeze each brake lever firmly; it should stop well before touching the handlebar. Rim brake pads have grooves that act as wear indicators, and the pads should be replaced when the grooves are almost gone. Worn pads can damage the rim and make braking weaker in wet weather.

Disc brakes are less affected by rain but are very sensitive to oil. If oil or lubricant gets on the rotor or pads, the brakes may squeal loudly and lose much of their stopping power. Clean a contaminated rotor with isopropyl alcohol and a clean cloth. Pads that have soaked up oil usually need to be replaced.

On cable-operated brakes, the cable slowly stretches and the lever travels further before the brake engages. Turn the barrel adjuster where the cable enters the brake lever to take up the slack. If the barrel adjuster has reached its limit, the cable needs to be retightened at the brake caliper.

Many countries legally require a white front light and a red rear light when riding at night, and a red rear reflector is often required as well. Check that the batteries are charged before setting off, and keep the lights clean so they can be seen clearly. Flashing modes can make a rider easier to notice in traffic, but some places require a steady front light.

A quick check before each ride can be remembered as ABC: air in the tyres, brakes working and chain clean and lubricated.
