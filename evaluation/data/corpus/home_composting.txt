Composting is the controlled breakdown of organic material by bacteria, fungi and small animals such as worms and insects. The result is a dark, nutrient-rich material that gardeners use to improve soil. A healthy compost pile needs four things: carbon-rich material, nitrogen-rich material, water and air.

Gardeners usually call carbon-rich material "browns" and nitrogen-rich material "greens". Browns include dry leaves, straw, shredded cardboard, sawdust from untreated wood and paper. Greens include vegetable and fruit scraps, coffee grounds, tea leaves, fresh grass clippings and plant trimmings. Despite the names, the color of the material does not matter; coffee grounds are brown in color but count as a green because they are rich in nitrogen.

Microorganisms work fastest when the pile has a carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of roughly 30 to 1. In practice, a simple rule is to add about two to three buckets of browns for every bucket of greens. Too many greens make the pile wet and smelly, while too many browns make it slow to decompose.

Chopping or shredding material into smaller pieces gives microorganisms more surface area to work on and speeds up the process. Large branches and thick stems can take years to break down unless they are cut up first.

A compost bin is not strictly required. Many people build an open heap in a corner of the garden, while others use closed plastic bins, wooden slatted boxes or rotating tumblers. Closed bins keep the pile tidier and can discourage animals, but every design follows the same biological principles.

Building and Maintaining the Pile

A pile of about one cubic meter is large enough to hold heat but small enough to turn by hand. Smaller piles lose heat quickly and decompose slowly, especially in cold weather. Start the pile on bare soil if possible so that worms and other organisms can move in from below.

Build the pile in layers, alternating browns and greens, and add water as you go. The material should feel as damp as a wrung-out sponge: moist to the touch but not dripping when squeezed. A pile that is too dry will stop decomposing, and a pile that is too wet will lose the air that aerobic microorganisms need.

Turning the pile with a garden fork every one to two weeks mixes the material and brings fresh oxygen to the center. Each time the pile is turned, move the material from the outside edges into the middle, where decomposition is most active.

In hot composting, the center of a well-built pile reaches between 55 and 65 degrees Celsius within a few days. Holding these temperatures for several days kills most weed seeds and many plant diseases. A compost thermometer with a long probe makes it easy to check the core temperature. When the temperature falls, turning the pile usually makes it heat up again.

Hot composting with regular turning can produce finished compost in two to three months. Cold composting, where material is simply added to a pile and left alone, needs much less work but can take six months to two years, and it does not reliably kill weed seeds.

Troubleshooting and Using Finished Compost

A pile that smells like rotten eggs or ammonia is usually too wet, contains too many greens or is not getting enough air. Mix in dry browns such as shredded cardboard or straw, and turn the pile to let oxygen back in. A properly working pile should smell earthy, not sour.

If the pile does not heat up, it may be too small, too dry or short of nitrogen. Add greens such as fresh grass clippings, moisten the material and make the pile larger if needed.

Some materials should stay out of a home compost pile. Meat, fish, bones, dairy products and oily foods attract rats and other pests and can create strong odors. Diseased plants and weeds that have gone to seed may survive a pile that does not get hot enough. Waste from cats and dogs can carry parasites and should never be used on food gardens. Charcoal ash and glossy printed paper can also introduce unwanted chemicals.

Compost is finished when it is dark, crumbly and has an earthy smell, and when the original materials are no longer recognizable. Spread a layer of two to five centimeters over garden beds, or mix it into the top layer of soil before planting. Compost improves soil structure, helps sandy soil hold water and helps heavy clay soil drain. Finished compost can also be screened through a mesh and mixed with sand and soil to make a potting mix for containers.
